Almost every business owner knows where they stand in Google. Almost nobody knows where they stand in Brave. Until recently there was no need to. Brave is a small search engine, mainly known from the browser of the same name.

Still, it is worth five minutes of your attention. Claude, the AI assistant from Anthropic, uses Brave to find your company when someone asks a question that requires searching the internet. And Claude is used a great deal by exactly the people who make decisions: business owners, buyers, advisers.

In this article we explain what we know for certain, what we do not know and what you can do with it.

## What we know for certain

Anthropic publishes a list of external parties that process data for its services. That is a legal obligation and therefore a reliable source. Brave Search has been on that list since March 2025, in the category for searching the web. That was the same week in which Claude was first able to search the internet itself.

It was noticed by developers who kept an eye on the list. [TechCrunch wrote about it](https://techcrunch.com/2025/03/21/anthropic-appears-to-be-using-brave-to-power-web-searches-for-its-claude-chatbot/), and a well known developer showed that a search in Claude and the same search in Brave returned the same sources.

In May 2026 a second party was added in the same category: turbopuffer, a company that builds search databases. Anthropic has not explained what exactly that party does in Claude's search process.

## What we do not know

In the interest of honesty: Anthropic has not explained in detail how Claude searches. We do not know how heavily Brave's results weigh, how many pages Claude reads and how it chooses what to quote. That can also change. A system can add another search service tomorrow.

So what we describe here is the current state, based on what is public and on what we see in our own measurements. Anyone who tells you they know exactly how Claude chooses knows more than Anthropic has told.

## Why this matters: Brave has its own index

An index is the collection of pages a search engine knows. Many small search engines do not have one. They buy their results from Google or Bing and put their own wrapper around them.

Brave does not. It has built its own index, separate from Google and Bing. That is why it is interesting to AI companies: it means they do not depend on a competitor.

For you that has one important consequence. Your position in Google says nothing about your position in Brave. We see companies on the first page in Google that only show up on the third in Brave. And we see small websites with clear, well structured pages that rank surprisingly high in Brave.

> You can be at the top of Google and still not exist for Claude.

## How to check where you stand in five minutes

Go to search.brave.com. You do not need to create an account and you do not need the Brave browser.

1. **Search for your company name.** Do you come first with your own website? Is the description correct? If not, Brave does not know you, or hardly.
2. **Search for your service and your town.** "Painter Zwolle", "bookkeeper freelancers Amersfoort". See who is on the first page. Those are the companies Claude chooses from.
3. **Search for a question customers ask.** "What does exterior painting cost per square metre?" See which pages come up and whether a company from your region is among them.
4. **Then ask Claude the same question.** Compare the names. You will see that a good share of them match.

Write down what you find. This is your starting point.

## What helps you get found in Brave

There is no sign up form that secures you a place. Brave discovers pages by itself. What you do control are the things that make finding and understanding easier.

### Do not block unknown visitors

This is the pitfall we run into most. Security packages and some hosting companies have a setting to keep out unknown or suspicious visitors. That sounds sensible. But Brave's visitor does not announce itself with a recognisable name of its own. A setting that is too strict therefore keeps it out, and you notice nothing.

As a rule of thumb: what Google cannot reach, Brave cannot reach either. Ask the party that manages your website whether blocks on visitors are switched on, and if so which ones.

### Make sure your text can be read straight away

A smaller search engine has fewer resources than Google to decipher complicated websites. Text that only appears after a script has run is missed sooner. The same goes for text in images.

A trial that always works: select all the text on your page, copy it and paste it into an empty document. What you see there is roughly what a search engine sees.

### Write pages that answer one question

Brave has less data about user behaviour than Google, and so it leans more heavily on what is on the page itself. A clear heading, a direct answer and a logical structure do a lot.

That is also why small websites sometimes come out above large ones here. A page that is precisely about "cost of replacing a gutter on a terraced house" beats a general page from a large company where that topic gets one line.

### Let others link to you

A search engine discovers new pages by following links. If there is no link to your website anywhere, the chance that a smaller search engine finds you is lower. A listing with a trade association, a supplier or a local directory therefore helps twice: it makes you findable and credible.

## How Claude handles the results

Searching is the first step. After that Claude reads a number of the pages it found and writes an answer. Three things play a part there.

**Claude quotes what is literally there.** It does not like to guess. If your page does not say you work in Deventer, Claude will not claim it, even if your office is there.

**Claude is careful with recommendations.** It often states what it bases its choice on and says so when it has little information. Companies with concrete, verifiable information therefore come out better than companies with only glowing words about themselves.

**Claude does not always search.** For general questions it answers from what the model already knows. For questions about local companies, prices and current matters it searches more often. For most business owners those are exactly the questions that count.

## Should I now start working separately for Brave?

No. It would be a waste to create a strategy of its own for every search engine, and it is not needed either.

The work you do to answer a question well helps everywhere. In Google, in Brave, in ChatGPT and in Gemini. What differs per system are the thresholds: one time it is your business profile, another time a technical block.

The practical conclusion is this:

- **Write well once.** Clear pages per question and per service.
- **Check in several places.** Do not look only in Google. Five minutes in Brave per quarter is enough to prevent surprises.
- **Measure per system.** If you are mentioned in ChatGPT and Gemini and not in Claude, Brave is the first place to look.

## Why should I care about Claude?

A fair question. ChatGPT has far more users.

There are two reasons to include Claude anyway. The first is the type of user. Claude is used a lot in business, inside companies and by people who work with it daily. For anyone who supplies other companies, that is a relevant group.

The second reason is spread. Nobody knows which system will be the largest in two years. Whoever builds their findability on one system is vulnerable. Whoever makes sure their website can be read and understood by every system does not need to worry about that.

## An example of what a difference looks like

A made up example, built from a pattern we come across. An accountancy firm ranks on the first page in Google for its town. The website is handsome and built with a lot of animation. The text of each service slides into view as you scroll.

In Brave, the firm shows up only for its own name. For "accountant for a limited company with staff" in its town, three smaller firms come first. Their websites are plain. Each has a page that explains what the service involves, what it costs per month and which kind of client it suits.

Ask Claude the same question and it names two of those three smaller firms. The larger firm is not weaker. Its text simply arrives too late for a visitor that reads the page as delivered, and its pages describe the firm instead of answering the question.

The fix does not require a new design. The text has to be in the page from the start, and each service needs a page that begins with the answer.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I sign up with Brave?

Brave offers a way to put forward an address, but that does not guarantee inclusion. The reliable route is making sure your website is reachable and that other websites link to you.

### Does it help if I use the Brave browser?

Not for your own findability. Your position does not improve because you use the browser yourself.

### Will this change?

Almost certainly. AI companies switch suppliers and add sources. That is why we advise against tuning your approach to one search engine. What remains is that a system has to be able to find, read and understand your page.

### How often should I look in Brave?

Once per quarter is enough for most companies, and again after any large change to your website. Look at three things each time: your name, your service with your town, and one question customers ask. If your position drops sharply between two checks, something technical has usually changed, such as a new security setting.
